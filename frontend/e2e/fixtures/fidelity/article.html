The Slow Gazette

Why the river town kept its ferry

By a contributor · 7 minute read

Every few years someone proposes a bridge. The plans arrive with handsome drawings, a budget that looks reasonable, and a promise that the crossing will take ninety seconds instead of nine minutes. And every few years the town votes, by a comfortable margin, to keep the ferry.

Visitors find this baffling. The ferry runs every twenty minutes, stops at eleven at night, and is sometimes late when the river is high. Yet ask anyone on the deck and you hear the same answer, phrased a dozen ways: the crossing is the point.

Nine minutes of nothing

The ferry is the only place in town where nobody is expected to do anything. Phones lose signal halfway across. The engine is loud enough that conversation needs leaning in. People read, stare at the water, or talk to someone they would never have met. A survey of regular passengers found that most could name at least one friend they had first spoken to on board.

“A bridge would get me home faster, and I would have nothing to tell anyone when I got there.”

What the numbers say

The ferry costs less to run than a bridge would cost to maintain, once the loan is counted. That argument rarely wins votes on its own, but it removes the strongest reason to build. The town's own accounts list three reasons the ferry keeps winning:

  1. It costs less over thirty years than the cheapest bridge proposal.
  2. Shops on both landings depend on people who wait for the next boat.
  3. The river is wide and soft-banked; a bridge would need deep piers.

None of this means the bridge will never come. It means the town has learned to ask a better question than “how fast can we cross?” — namely, what the crossing is for.
